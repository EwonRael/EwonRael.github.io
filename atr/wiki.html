American Type Revival

Technical Information

Overview

The goal of ATR is to make high quality digital reproductions of analogue type. This page documents the standards and procedures that should be followed in making these digital fonts. The goal is to provide consistency within the ATR font library, as well as provide the highest quality font files possible. Having standardized procedures helps accomplish both these goals.

Digital fonts and analogue type are two fundamentally different mediums with unique advantages and limitations. Digital revivals should aim to both accurately represent their source material, while also providing a practical and usable digital typeface. These two goals are often in direct conflict with each other. This document is, in part, an explanation of how to navigate making this compromise.

Choosing Source Material

Choosing a primary source
The primary source for each font is a specimen from an antique type catalog. This provides not only references for what the glyphs look like, but information such as the name of the font style. Supplementary material can and should be used, such as other type specimens or reference books, but for each font there should be a specific type specimen book that can be cited as the primary source. This is because type foundries changed information about individual typefaces (such as the name of the typeface) year to year. As much as possible the primary source for a font family should be the same.

When choosing a primary source, it's important to not only consider the quality (scan resolution, completeness), but also the needs and expectations of digital fonts. Digital fonts expect a font family with Regular, Italic, Bold, and Bold Italic versions. If two sources for the same typeface are of equal quality in terms of their ability to be digitizes, but one is named in such a way that it lends itself better to digital typography, go with the source that's better suited in terms of naming conventions

In fact, it's perhaps sometimes desirable to choose a worse quality source as the primary source if it's better suited in terms of naming conventions.

Making sure it's public domain
Legally speaking it's basically impossible to run afoul when it comes to choosing source material. This is because in the US, letterforms are ineligible for copyright protection. As long as you are using analogue source material (literally the entire point of the project) no one can sue.

That being said, it is possible to commit faux pas in the design world. You don't want to come off like you're actively competing with anyone. For this reason it's better to use public domain sources. These are sources from before . It's also better to use sources from the American Type Founder because they've gone defunct in 1993 and no longer exist as a company to be competing with. Try and have source material be at least one of those two things if not both.

Unfortunately there are ghouls (Monotype) that maintain trademark protection on certain font names, meaning fonts sometimes have to be misnamed in the metadata to comply with trademark law. This doesn't affect what can be used as source material, but it does effect naming conventions in the metadata.



Metadata

One of the surprisingly difficult and frustrating aspects of this project is getting the naming convention of historical typefaces to play well with modern digital font metadata needs. Metal type designers had no knowledge of the frustration that computers would bring, and those making the digital font standards had no respect for older naming conventions.

The compromise is to make the metadata that is most likely to be read by computers as normal as possible, even if it's not historically accurate, and to make the metadata most likely to be read by humans as historically accurate as possible, even if it will confuse people.

What do computers want?
Computers really only expect a font family to consist of a maximum of four specific styles: Regular, Italic, Bold, and Bold Italic. The computer doesn't understand the idea of a Light, Medium, or Black weight. The computer doesn't get what condensed or expanded is. And the computer sure as hell doesn't know what cursive or inline or old-style is. I know it seems like the computer might understand some of these things because there's ways of tagging the metadata to indicate if a font is medium weight, or condensed, but the computer is much stupider than you think and you will confuse it if you don't treat it like it's stupid.

This means that, to accommodate for the stupidness of the computer, the basic metadata should only ever report that a font is Regular, Italic, Bold, or Bold Italic.

Sort everything into buckets, and look for groups of four.
When going through a type specimen, sort everything into those four buckets, defaulting to Regular. Only put fonts into Bold or Italic buckets if they are explicitly named those things in the source material. If something is close to being Bold or Italic but not explicitly those things, they are Regular.

Fatface → Regular
Heavy → Regular
Cursive → Regular

The computer also gets really really confused if there's more than one Regular font in the same font family. Therefore, each style gets a new font family in the metadata. Cloister Cursive is obviously part of the Cloister family, but in the metadata it's a separate font-family called Cloister Cursive and it gets a style of Regular of course.

This means from a metadata perspective some fonts that are clearly part of the same family will be categorized as separate families. However, when possible, groups of the four styles computers understand should be made. This also means that sometimes fonts that might seem like they should belong to separate font families should be grouped together

In old type catalogs sometimes the regular weight version of fonts are given a modifier word like Commercial or Expanded which don't appear in the bold counterparts. It is preferable to create the groups of expected four, therefore regular style fonts with modifier words should be put into families without the modifier words if this allows for the inclusion of bold counterpart. Only drop the modifier words in the family name if it helps to create a group of four.

The dropping of modifier words is only to make the font family metadata play nice. Retain the modifier words in the font name metadata.

It's also important that the created group of four feel as though they belong together. American Type Founders acquired many smaller type companies over the years, and in in doing so acquired an eclectic group of typefaces that sometimes share a name (especially in the case of typefaces named after historical designers) but that don't share underlying design logic. It is possible often through research to ascertain if two different fonts were designed as part of the same family or just happen to share a name, but there is also a subjective element to it. When creating groups of four ask yourself, do these really feel like the same font family? If the answer is no then don't group them together, even if this creates an unruly number of unique font families.

Ground families using the type specimens as a reference.
Which version of a font is normal? There are going to be instances where it's tempting to make the normal version of a font something different from what the source material gestures towards. For example, the typeface Hobo has two styles in ATF catalogs: Series and Light. It's very tempting to make the Light version the Regular version in the metadata and to make the Series version the Bold in the metadata, because then you don't have to have two separate font families.

However, as tempting as this is, it's clear in the source material that the Series version is the default, and the Light version is a deviation, therefore we must make the Series version the Regular in the metadata.

What does this even mean if both have to be separate font families, and both have to be labeled as Regular in the metadata? It means that the grounded font has a font family name that doesn't include the specific style. In the case of Hobo Series this will just be given the font family of Hobo in the metadata.

Series means Regular
In old type catalogs the word Series is often used to mean what font family means today. This means two things: first, if a font is labeled Series it's always the normal version of the font, no matter what. Secondly, it means that the word Series can (and should) be dropped from the metadata because it's not the name of the specific style. This is the one and only case where dropping words from the name of a font is acceptable.

The font name: computers and people
In digital fonts there are two font names: one for computers, and one for humans. The name for humans provides us with the perfect opportunity to make the metadata reflect the source material. The postscriptFullName is the name for humans, and this should, as much as possible, preserve the font name in the source material. This includes preserving the order information is given in, even if it goes against contemporary convention. For example, in the ATF catalog the light version of Hobo is called Light Hobo so the postscriptFullName should be Light Hobo despite the fact that today it is more common to put the family name first and the style second, making Hobo Light more normal.

The name for computer is the postscriptFontName. Most of the time this isn't going to be seen by people. The name for computers should always start with the family name with spaces removed, followed by a hyphen, followed only by the style information with spaces removed. The reason the family name goes first is so that when sorted alphabetically fonts of the same family will be grouped together.

Not all computers are stupid
Font metadata, like a lot of things in this world, is an imperfect system with little patches and fixes added to it. At some point people realized that it would be nice if, for example, lighter weight styles, or condensed versions of fonts, could be grouped into the same font family as the regular counterpart, instead of having them all be separate families.

This can't be reflected in the existing metadata, because a lot of programs would have a conniption, but new metadata fields were added to explain to programs that aren't so pathetically stupid that an individual style is part of a larger family. The relevant fields are called openTypeNamePreferredFamilyName and openTypeNamePreferredSubfamilyName respectively.

These programs are smart enough to understand that different weights and widths (condensed or expanded versions) are still part of the same font family, but they aren't smart enough to understand things like inline or cursive variants. Therefore, if and only if the font variant is either a different weight or condensed/expanded use openTypeNamePreferredFamilyName to indicated which family it belongs to. Otherwise (as would be the case for a Cursive or Outline variant) keep it as a separate family.

Make sure the width and weight are properly tagged in the metadata, otherwise these stupid programs will still get confused.

Avoiding getting sued by Monotype
Even though the design of fonts in impossible to copyright, and stuff from 1923 is public domain, there's still the annoying issue of Trademark. When ATF went defunct their trademarks got scooped up and most (maybe all?) of them ended up with Monotype, which is known for being a bit litigious when it comes to enforcing their intellectual property.

Monotype keeps a handy spreadsheet of all their trademarks. If a font name appears on the list, it must be renamed to something similar, but legally distinct. For example, Monotype owns Engravers, so in the font is renamed Engraving.

It sucks because it's not historically accurate. Notes can and should be made both on the site and also in the about section of the metadata indicating the name of the original typeface.

Including the foundry initials.
Finally, the font metadata should include ATR at the end to indicate it a American Type Revival release. This should always appear at the end of anything that includes the name of the typeface. It appears at the end so as to not disrupt alphabetizing.

Metrics

Basic Font Metrics
The following things are standardized for all ATR fonts:

Metadata Field Content
Em Size 2000
Ascent 1600
Descent 400
Capital Height 1300

The reference material should be scaled in such a way that the capital height of the actual drawn letters matches the reported capital height in the metadata.

The X Height in the metadata should reflect the x-height of the drawn letters, which will change on a font by font basis. Not all the source material shares the same x-height.

This is not true, however, for the Descent metadata field, which will remain the same regardless of the drawn descender height.

The invisible grid: subunits for widths
Historically speaking, the widths of metal type letters were limited. Letter could not be any width ever, but instead were always chosen from a preset collection of potential widths. The potential widths letters could be all shared a fractional relationship to each other. For example, a lowercase m might be twice the width of a lowercase s. Or a r might be 2/3rds the width of a u.



You're very smart, and you already understand this intuitively.

Because all these widths are fractionally related to each other, we can derive a sort of sub-unit that makes them up. In the case of Times (shown above), the r is 6 sub units wide and the u is 9 wide.

In the case of text fonts, it is highly advantageous for this sort of subunits to be reverse engineered, and reproduced in the digital font. Especially for roman (upright) book (not bold) fonts. This is both to preserve historical accuracy, a digital font with this sort of sub unit width system will more accurately reflect the metal type its based on, but it also aids in legibility.

Dealing with a lack of info
However, determining the underlying system from photo copies of type specimens is difficult if not outright impossible. This is because, like most of elements of the font, the spacing varies from one point size to the next. Many letters are printed outside of the context of words, so there is literally no information to go off of.

To accommodate for this information deficit, calculations have been made to determine the mostly likely scenario. A collection of over 100 fonts where the sub-unit size was known were analyzed, and the most common widths for each letter were calculated.

When digitizing a new font, start by using the following widths. If a letter width appears too small, increase its size by one subwidth unit. If it appears too big decrease its size. Guess and check.

Please note: the following table shows what is most common in roman, book, serif fonts. These are the most important to get right when it comes to legibility. When digitizing a family start with the upright book weight.

You will notice that the subwidth unit used is 100. This makes things easy. This subunit width was chosen to work well with a capital height of 1300. The subwidth unit being 100 is not random, and it should not be changed.

For weights that will be used in laying out text, tabular digits should be the default, with lining digits being offered as a stylistic alternative if desired.

When to not use subunits for widths
The point of the subunit system is to reveal the underlying logic that's already there. It is to make the font more beautiful and legible. When applying the system, it should feel like the letters want to go in their respective boxes. In cases where you feel like applying the system is forcing letters to conform to space they don't want to occupy, it is always better to abandon the system.

The system should either be applied for all letters, or be abandoned entirely, but it should not be partially applied. If one or two letters are unhappy with the system, but the others work quite well, consider adjusting the widths of the unhappy letters (in terms of what's drawn).

Remember that with analog type there is variation between point sizes, and different point sizes have different relative widths and spacing. The digital font is always a compromise between what are essentially different alphabets. Realistically, different letters are going to be drawn using different point size material, and the end result is going to be an eclectic mix of widths.

This reminder serves both to explain why the system might not apply well, but also allow for the widening or narrowing of specific glyphs guilt free.

Because this system aids in legibility it is less important in display fonts. Always try to apply the system first, but try a bit harder to apply it in fonts that might be used to layout large paragraphs of text.



Drawing the Letters

Now that we've done the difficult work of identifying our source material and preparing our workspace, so to speak, we can get down to the actual meat and potatoes of drawing the letters. In theory we could start by tracing the highest quality (i.e. largest point size) letter available and work to harmonize the alphabet second, but it's actually better to make some modifications to the source material first.

Understanding different point sizes (and preparing the source material)
There are some obvious, and some not so obvious, differences between different point sizes. Understanding how the alphabet changes at different sizes is crucial.

Below are three digitizations of the Century Expanded typeface based off of different point size material.

Century Expanded at 72 points

Century Expanded at 12 points

Century Expanded at 6 points

All three are from the same page of the same catalog. Looking at this, it's immediately obvious that the letters are getting bolder. Ink spreads, and at smaller printing sizes this has a bigger effect on the look of the letters.

Ink spreading is a limitation of the technology of analogue type. It's actually a limitation that still exists today as presumably someone might print out documents using a ATR font at some point. Because this is a flaw and not representative of the intent of the designer it should be accounted for and undone in the digital font.

Depending on your workflow you might prefer to account for this inside the font making program, but it's also possible to unbold text in a photo editor (like Gimp). In either case, measure the stroke width from a large (72?) point size and compare that to same stroke from a smaller point size. Subtract from the smaller sources until it matches the larger source.

What is less obvious, but actually much more important, is how the letters get wider at smaller point sizes. This is not due to ink spread. This was an intentional design decision. Comparing the 72 and 12 point fonts the difference in wideness is so subtle that it might seem unimportant. But it adds up.

With in only four letters the 12 point (blue) is an entire stroke width wider than the 72 point (brown) font. Imagine how much this compounds over paragraphs.

The reason this is so very important is because we are trying to reverse engineer the subunits for the width. The entire subunit system is thrown off if the width of letter doesn't match what it's expecting, so pulling from different point sizes without accounting for this is devastating.

Intuitively, one might assume that the widths of the letters printed at larger point sizes are superior to those printed at smaller point sizes. After all, the larger point sizes are higher quality in almost every regard. Maybe we should shrink the widths of the smaller printed letters to match the larger ones?

However, unlike most differences between the smaller sizes and larger sizes, the widths of letters is not a result of the limitations of the technology. This is an intentional design decision. The slight increase in letter widths at the smaller text sizes give them more breathing room, and aid in legibility.

This means that in the case of fonts that are display faces and would never be used to lay out long paragraphs of text it is okay and appropriate to squish the smaller point size letters to match the larger point size, but in the case of fonts that might be used to lay out text, it is preferable to go with the widths of smaller point sizes.

This is one of those moments where digital font design is a compromise. It's impossible to simultaneously represent what letters look like at large and small point sizes, so one has to be chosen over the other, and it is better to sacrifice a small amount of beauty at display sizes to maintain maximum legibility at text sizes.

For this reason, the subunit system is designed to work with the widths found in 12 point fonts. 100 being the width of one subunit is a calculation made for the letter widths found at 12 points. If you are working on a display typeface and you want to use a subunit width suited for 72 point sources, use a subunit width of 95. I know earlier I said that it's always 100, but I lied.

When the subunit system is applied, 72 points has too much space between letter, 12 points looks perfect, and 6 points is bleeding outside the edge.

The number of subunits making up each of the letters in the Century typeface is known and applied in the above example. The 72 point source appears too roomy, and the 6 point source appears too cramped. If the number of subunits was unknown and was being based off of the guess and check method we would guess the wrong number of subunits if using the unmodified 72 point size source material.

All of this means that none of the source material should be seen as authoritative. The smaller point size material should be seen as too bold and too low fidelity, and the larger point size material should be seen as too thin.

Increase the width of the larger point size material to match the widths of the 12 point letters.

A system of compromises
Drawing the letters is where the art of type revival comes into play. There are many possible decisions and routs that can be taken, and the right decision depends on individual preference, and also changes on a font-by-font basis. Here are a couple things to keep in mind:

  • The logic and system of interpretation should be internally consistent within fonts and font families
  • Having a nice looking font should be the top priority
  • Expressing the intention of the designer should be a top priority
  • More fidelity is better
  • Fonts should reflect their source material

These are listed in order of most important to least important. Ideally font revivals can satisfy all to a very high degree, but there are circumstances where these priorities may come into conflict.

Obviously if we went with the highest quality source material and made no efforts to harmonize it we would have some letters based on smaller point sizes that end up bolder and wider than the letters based on larger point size material. But there's also less obvious ways these priorities come into conflict.

Sometimes designers made design decisions knowing they will get rounded off or muddled by the analog type process (for example having a sharp acute point). If we literally interpret what the designer was doing it would look bad. In these moments it's better to go against what the designer did in service of nice looking letters

There are different ways of dealing with these circumstances. One method, which can be very effective, is to round off all sharp edges. Another method is to cut the tips off of sharp points off. What looks best depends on the individual font style. The important thing is that there should be some kind of consistent logic in the font.

This is a close up of the typeface "Tiffany Gothic," later renamed "Typo." If we interpret the sharp points literally it creates a sort of dazzling effect that looks bad. This is the most literal interpretation of the design.

One option might be to cup the tips of sharp points off.

Another option might be to round off all sharp points.

It's not important than how these sorts of situations are handled is consistent between the entire ATR catalog. What's important is that there is consistency inside a font family, and that whatever decision is made it's in service of the font.

Try and identify potential issues early and come up with a system so you don't have to go through after already drawing letters rounding off corners to make them harmonize with a problem letter.

The specific example given of sharp points is not the only sort of problem that can arise with literally interpreting the design. It is an illustrative example. It is impossible to anticipate every sort of problem, but when problems arise think about what sorts of compromises can and should be made.

Dealing with low fidelity sources
Small point sizes have less fidelity than larger point sizes. This means some of the details are going to be lost. It's best to retain as much detail as possible, so finding the best quality source possible is a must. Consult different type catalogs as well as any printed material you can find in the typeface you're working on. It's possible to sometimes find/create more source material. Public libraries sometimes have books that aren't digitized. Sometimes people working at museums will email you scans of source material. Buying a rare book is expensive, but emailing someone that works at a British museum is free.

It feels unethical, and maybe it's not the sort of thing you'd want to admit in public, but it's possible to use other digitizations as reference material. Big type foundries often have digitized their entire type catalog and they have resources you can only dream of.

However, when using various sources, it's always super important to try and investigate how those sources differ from the source you've chosen to be the primary source. Many typefaces have been adapted to many different technologies over the years, and often type foundries use a more recent adaptation, or have themselves made modifications to the text.

Here we see "Century Expanded" as it appears in a 1923 American Type Founders catalog.

Here is a digital font called "Century Expanded" as sold by Linotype. It is bolder and the angles of the serifs is steeper.

Overlaying the two we can see that the digital font (blue) has both shorter ascenders and longer decenders.

Figuring out the exact provenance of these changes is not super important or relevant (or even always possible), but it's clear that the underlying design logic is different. If you were to, for whatever reason, use the modern digital font as a reference for the letter g without taking into account the fact that the descenders are different, you're going to end up with a letter that uses different design logic than the rest of the alphabet.

I don't care if you copy modern fonts. It's legal as long as you're only looking at the shape of the letters and not looking at the font software. Maybe it's unethical or frowned upon but I don't care. The issue is that these fonts don't preserve the original designs well. They are poor sources when it comes to historicity.

Often times they are just very poor quality. The letters are lumpy or weird looking. Large foundries have a lot of resources and in theory (and sometimes do) make incredibly well researched and executed revivals. But more often than not it's some freshman designer with little experience or pay going through a chunk of 600 fonts to digitize by Monday and the source material is photocopies of photocopies.

What brilliant design logic governs the shape of the curves of this digital Hobo revival? Is there some kind of genius logic at play that makes the "p" stick out funny?

No, when we consult the original the letters are much more harmonious. The digital font is just poorly done.

Maybe it's judge-worthy to use modern sources as reference material. Who am I to say? But you will definitely look like an idiot if you copy the mistakes of modern digital fonts. Be very careful and discerning.

Drawing what's not there
Modern fonts require symbols that were either rare in the past, or didn't exist at all. For example, euros as a currency only went into circulation after the turn of the millennium. It's one of the most important currencies on earth, and any digital font without a euro sign is incomplete, but there is also definitely no historical euro signs to drawn on.

Not only are there many modern signs that didn't exist back in the day, the ethos when it came to symbols was different in the era of movable type. A digital font can include hundreds of symbols and the expense and difficulty of installing it remains unchanged, but if you are working with literal pieces of lead, including hundreds of symbols that almost never get used in the off chance that you'll need an at symbol at some point would be insanity.

The attitude back then was to sell generic versions of rare symbols (like the at sign, which did exist before emails) and customers could buy and use only what they needed.

The result is many of the symbols that today are considered necessary either didn't exist at all, or didn't have a typeface specific version.

The most historically accurate thing to do (and in some cases the preferable thing) would be to design generic versions of symbols, or to not include certain symbols at all. However, there are moments where a generic version of a symbol is going to be very distracting and disruptive to the flow of the text. In these cases it is better to design new symbols to match the design language of the original font.

A copyright symbol (©) is always a bit disruptive, and users don't typically expect that they will blend in with text. A generic copyright symbol is fine.

On the other hand a generic euro symbol (€) would stand out a bit more. More importantly, designers almost always drew font-specific dollar ($) and cents (¢) signs, so if we extrapolate the logic than font-specific currency signs are a must.

In general a good rule of thumb, is that if you would expect the glyph to look condensed in a condensed font it should get a custom version, and if you wouldn't expect the glyph to look condensed in a condesed font than a generic shape is fine.

The important thing is to try and keep track of what is based on historical designs, so when the font gets published we can make it clear what's invented and what's historical

The agnostic quotes
There are some glyphs, such as agnostic quotes, that are impossible to draw well. These didn't exist in historical typefaces, so there's no record to draw from, but they are also vital in digital fonts. It is true in the narrow case of agnostic quotes that there is no way to design the glyph well, but it should also be understood as a general rule. Sometimes there is no winning.

How do I know when it's done?
There are so many thousands of glyphs that in theory you can keep expanding a font indefinitely. However, the Google Fonts Basic Latin character set covers all the glyphs that should be seen as necessary for a complete font.



Kerning

Kerning is an area with one of the most dramatic differences between what was possible historically and what is possible today. With metal type it was basically impossible to adjust the space between two letters. Today infinite kerning pairs can be programmed and controlled with a basically infinite level of precision.

Historical kerning
It basically didn't exist at all. Sometimes specific letter pairs that create big problems were sold as kerning pairs, but often this was limited to very few problem pairs, and were not available at every point size.

No body would argue that the space between the Y and the o look good here. And this is from a type catalog who's point is to sell type.

Less extreme but still bad.

Type designers back in the day employed many techniques to mitigate the limitations of the time. A lot of this mitigation exists in the design of the letters themselves. But even the most skilled designer is going to have a hard time avoiding these sorts of situations.

The most historically accurate thing to do would be to have little to no kerning at all and just allow these sorts of problems to exist. But in extreme cases this creates kerning so bad that it's distracting, even to type novices, and even though it accurately represents the historical reality, I have a hard time believing that anyone was happy with the space between letters back in the day, it was just something they had to deal with.

Overdoing it
It tempting because we have a lot of power, to use it. Type designers today often get really obsessive with the subtleties of the space between letters. There are some letter pairs, like a n followed by a u where the design of the letters creates more space between them than other letter pairings. It is true that if your primary concern is having optically equal space between all letters than decreasing the space between n and u is a must.

However, making small adjustments to every single letter pair creates unintended consequences. There is a rhythm to the flow of letters, and getting too concerned with the details might make individual letter pairs look better, but it will disrupt the rhythm of the words and paragraphs.

You don't actually want the space between letters to be equal. Even in cases where having kerning is probably advisable, adjusting the spacing until things are equal is going to look bad.

Here is the font Cooper with no kerning at all. This is one of the classic problem areas where there is going to be too much space between the letters.

Here the letter spacing has been adjusted to make things equal. It looks worse than the version with no kerning at all.

This is the ideal amount of kerning. It's less than half the amount we would do if we were going for actual equality.

The problem is you can't look at things too much. You want to obsess over details. You want to make it look good. Don't do that. Be like an absentee father or something. Take zero interest in anything unless it looks so so bad it can't be ignored.

Maintaining the rhythm
Remember that thing about how back in the day there was a sort of subunit system governing the spacing between letters? Well if there's a sort of invisable but predicable rhythm governing the spacing between all the letters it's probably preferable to maintain that sort of rhythm. Adjusting kerning pairs has the potential to disrupt that rhythm.

This is easy to do. The sub units are 100 wide, so kerning pairs should be made in amounts divisible by 100. Doing this has a secondary advantage because it discourages the sort of neurotic fine-tuning that can be a seductive waste of time.

Keep everything separate
With digital kerning we have the ability to lump groups of letters together, and there are situations where this is intuitive and appealing. For example a D really appears to be the same on its right side as an O. Isn't this a classic case of letters we can treat the same from a kerning perspective? That would be true if the guiding philosophy of the font was different, but remember, we're putting all these letters onto imaginary metal blocks, and its possible (and even likely) that the amount of space between the end of the letter and the end of the block is different letter to letter. They might appear the same, but they're not.

Without any context it makes sense to think that we can treat O and D as being the same on the right for kerning purposes.

But if we can see the metal blocks they're inside of, we realize that the space between the stroke and the edge of the block (marked in red) is very different.

There's also the devastating reality that these curves aren't the same. O is much thinner a curve than D.

Make separate kerning classes for every letter, even if they seem like they could be grouped together.
