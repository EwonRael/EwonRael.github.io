About the font

Hobo is a true American classic. Created by Morris Fuller Benton during his tenure as head of design at American Type Founders, it’s a rare example of an Art Nouveau sans serif. Its blend of warm and organic shapes characteristic of the Art Nouveau movement, and simple, unornamented and unpretentious sans serif letterforms (something very much not characteristic of Art Nouveau) has earned it an enduring place in the American graphic design vernacular.

Hobo was first released in 1910, and was popular enough to warrant the introduction of a second, light version released in 1915. Throughout the 20th century it found its way onto TV title sequences, magazine advertisements, and album covers. Hobo became particularly popular in the food and beverage industry and can be found on restaurant signs, pizza boxes, and grocery staples.

Although Hobo is massively popular, its friendlier and more casual tone has earned it a bit of a reputation for being unprofessional, and consequently, digital revivals can be mixed in their quality and execution. It’s also one of the frustratingly large number of font families with individual styles that never made the leap from metal to digital type. To my knowledge, a faithful digital revival of the Light version has never been made, until now.
